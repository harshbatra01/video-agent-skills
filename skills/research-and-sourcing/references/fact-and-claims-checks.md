# Fact checks and claims checks

How to check scripts, client briefs, papers and on-screen numbers, and how to keep ads and posts clear of claims
trouble. Legal facts were gathered in Sept to Oct 2026 for UK and US work. They are research, not legal advice: when
a campaign is risky, say so and let the client get proper advice.

## 1. Rules that never bend
- **Never fabricate:** no invented stats, poll results, quotes, headlines, DMs, trades, wins, testimonials or AI answers
  (AI answers must be real model replies built on real data, such as real form figures). Why: one exposed fake ends a
  page's credibility; the platform will not catch it, but screenshots will.
- **Make the honest version of a viral lie, never the lie.** A fake "X won the award" post did 14.8M views on 21K
  followers; the honest version was "one name is at 63%", with a date. A newspaper check (Jun 2026) found 778 of 1,105
  TikToks for one prediction-market brand showed trades apparently filmed on copies of the app, and a fake "53% odds"
  screenshot of a market that never existed went viral (Jan 2026). A client brief asked for a format that fakes a
  trader's DM with an AI-written reply: we skipped it and said why.
- **Say no and give the honest version in the same reply.** When the boss suggested an invented poll stat, Claude
  declined and offered three real alternatives (numbers side by side, a Story poll, real quotes). Why: the page's
  credibility and the team's trust outlast one post.
- **No unchecked number in any draft,** not even in an example post (an unverified hashtag count slipped into one:
  replaced by a placeholder until read live). Never type a number by hand into a data post: read it from the source and
  time-stamp it.
- **Real settled trades only, losses as honest as wins, realistic money** (one client set limits on the sizes shown).

## 2. Checking the client's own brief, claims and links
Why: a post built on a wrong client claim is the agency's risk, and the client may not know the claim is wrong.
- Read every client file and follow every link (examples, Drive folders, creator guides). Note dead links, folders
  behind a sign-in ("I dont have acesss either"), and links that point at the wrong thing.
- Check every product claim and the CTA line against the live product, its fee page, its leaderboard and its docs.
  Example (a prediction-market app, Oct 2026): "zero fees" was not accurate (opening was free, but about 0.05% to 0.14%
  was charged on close or settle since about 5 Sep 2026); "world's fastest" had no benchmark (only the underlying
  chain's 0.2 s median latency existed); a star trader named in the brief was not on the leaderboard; a "demo format"
  link went to an unrelated clip; one example account was gone.
- List each problem once for the boss, with a fix. Keep the client's CTA in one config line so a fix is one edit.
- Check the brief's story against the client's own site: a brand site told a key year's story differently from the
  script (no "two weeks").
- Check the client's terms against the audience you target: one client's terms blocked the UK and US while the
  agency's pages defaulted to a UK audience. Raise it once; if the user says the client handles it ("countries are taken
  care of dont worry"), never raise it again.
- Check where the editor works: India's Online Gaming Act 2025 (s.6) bans making ads for online money games, with
  prison terms of up to 2 years. Raised once; the user said it was handled; the boss owns that call.
- Leave out a company's best-case claims unless a neutral source backs them, and say in chat what you left out.
- Rules seen in a supplement brief, as a model of what to look for in any health brief:
  - no claim that the product prevents, treats, cures or speeds recovery from illness;
  - never call competitors' products "synthetic", say they "cannot be absorbed" or are "urinated out";
  - no blanket "better absorption", bioavailability or efficacy claims without finished-product evidence;
  - no claims about weight-loss-drug side effects or muscle preservation;
  - wording for children only after the label, the minimum age and the serving guidance are checked;
  - a founder's historic views (soil depletion, "missing links" in the diet) are heritage, not present-day fact;
    archived medical anecdotes stay archive.

## 3. Checking a script before you source for it
- Fact-check the script against dated sources before sourcing archive. Why: wrong-event footage is both a credibility
  and a claims risk, and sourcing time is wasted on a line that will change.
- What one check found: the product had a different name in the year the story is set; "the season was a write-off"
  overstated it (she won races until 30 Aug that year); a famous collapse happened after a race, not from the illness
  in the script; one event was confirmed but no footage of it exists anywhere.
- Two numbers can both be right: "held the record 17 years" (13 Oct 2002 to 13 Oct 2019) and "the time stood 16 years"
  measure different things. Check what each one measures before you "correct" it.
- Age lines expire: "I'm 52" is wrong after the talent's next birthday. Put the expiry date in the build notes.
- Flag lines the script states more strongly than the sources ("shut down" where the sources say "restricted").
- Never pass off another event as the one the line names. If the user accepts a stand-in ("if not of exact same race
  then any race that would fit"), mark it in the footage map ("stand-in", "not [year], mood only"), say so in the
  report, and avoid a caption that names the event over different footage. One caption named a championship over
  footage of a different race: it stayed flagged as an open item.
- Keep a list of claim risks to raise when compliance is unparked (section 13).

## 4. Numbers against papers and data
- Fact-check every on-screen number against the paper, and list the flags in the build notes. Why: an evidence
  channel's whole promise is "the raw evidence".
- Per study, check: n (and the split), the comparison (against sham or baseline), the units, the duration, the
  protocol against the product's current claims, the funder, and what "independent" really means.
- Examples (light-therapy hair caps, Oct 2026):
  - "about nineteen extra hairs" = a 2026 meta-analysis, +18.56 hairs/cm2 against sham: the wording held.
  - A 2014 trial: n = 269 (128 men, 141 women); laser +18.4 to +25.7 against sham +1.6 to +9.4 terminal hairs/cm2 at
    26 weeks; partly funded by the maker (a line found only in the full text).
  - A 2017 trial used 30 minutes every other day for 17 weeks in 44 women, while today's product page says "6 minutes a
    day": the protocol does not match the product claim.
  - A 2019 study the script called "more independent" was run by a university that built its own cap: independent only
    of the cap sellers.
- Get the full text: PMC article pages put scripts behind a reCAPTCHA, but Europe PMC's REST API returns the same full
  text (methods, funding lines); PubMed pages screenshot fine for a source card. Quote from the full text when the
  screenshot lacks the line.
- Numbers said by guests and presenters: check every one before it goes on screen (one guest misstated a company's sale
  price by about half a billion).
- Data posts:
  - The number viewers will see in the app beats the number you polled (a market polled at 52% while the app showed
    70%): caption with the app's own label.
  - A placeholder value is not data (a price of exactly 0.5 with no order book is an untraded placeholder: never
    publish it).
  - Sanity sums: the sides of a multi-way market must add to about 90% to 106%, or skip it.
  - Thin live data flickers (a team shown at 66% while 1-3 down): use filtered values and the resolved result.
  - Keep a number identical on every slide (a benchmark account showed 55%, 51% and 50.8% for the same thing in one
    post: a mistake that invites comments).
  - Time-stamp every number ("Price from [app], 1 minute before kick-off").
- A game format's "draw" is one weighted draw with a logged seed (weight by the real chances, seed with the date): say
  it is a draw, so the result is reproducible and not staged.

## 5. Names, quotes and identity
- Confirm every name outside the audio: transcription mangles names, and two big models agreeing means nothing
  ("Robbie" was "Rabbi"). Correct a name everywhere at once and check that zero old copies remain; if a spelling stays
  unsure, pick one, use it everywhere and flag it. Re-transcription tricks: premiere-ae-editing media-toolkit.md
  section 5.
- Quotes and headlines word for word, from the live page or the full text.
- When the client wrote the cut or outline: verify every quoted line against the tape (verbatim), confirm speakers on
  the mic tracks, list every difference, state the real runtime, and correct quietly without re-authoring their
  structure.
- Translate foreign-language lyrics and hooks before building on them ("¿Y qué fue?" is a taunt, "so what happened?",
  and it was matched to a real shrug).
- Never imply a stand-in is a named person: a photo of a lookalike needs a licensed photo of the real person with a
  credit pill, or an "Illustration" label, and repeats should vary (a client rule).
- Never hint at material a guest asked to keep out.
- Confirm who is who before labelling a face (news-and-story-research.md section 9).

## 6. Facts on screen in edits and posts
- Every number on screen is a checked fact, music edits included. Keep a facts-to-check list per edit and ask the user
  to double-check before posting. Examples from one edit: a birth date, a home postcode, a record's exact age ("16 years
  362 days"), a shirt number, an age at a title, a draft position.
- Frame 0 shows final, true numbers, never a number mid-count.
- Date-bound posts expire ("today", "tonight", "in 12 days"): refresh the number or rebuild as _v2 before posting.
- Do not imply ratings or results you cannot back: in a "1 star reviews" ad light one star, not five.
- Titles and thumbnails make claims too: flag any number a title or thumbnail cannot back before it is written.

## 7. Health and supplement claims (UK CAP Code and ASA)
- **CAP Code points that decide supplement ads:** 15.1.1 only authorised health claims, in the prescribed wording and
  under their conditions of use; 15.6.2 nothing that implies a food supplement prevents, treats or cures illness; 15.8
  nothing that implies a normal diet is not enough; 3.1 nothing misleading.
- **A genuine testimonial is not a separate route for claims** (ASA ruling on Real Health Supplements Ltd, upheld
  24 Dec 2025): customer reviews that linked supplements to recovery from a condition (back pain, coming off an
  antidepressant) were treated as the advertiser's own claims (15.6.2), and inviting reviews as a route to condition
  information was misleading (3.1). A disclaimer such as "this is the user's own experience" does not remove the
  implied claim.
- **Testimonials:** hold documentary evidence that each one is genuine and keep the person's contact details; never
  edit one so that it changes meaning or seems to be about a newer product.
- **The high-risk pattern:** condition, then product, then improvement, recovery, stopping medication or cure. A
  headline, an image or the surrounding copy can create an implied medicinal claim even when every sentence is careful.
  Safer material: factual background, lifestyle context, the product routine, genuine subjective experience, factual
  life events.
- **A star's results tied to a product:** an ASA ban was reported on 30 Sep 2026 for a meal-replacement brand's ad whose
  story tied an athlete's race win to the product (reported by City AM; not checked on the ASA site). A "comeback from
  illness, thanks to the product" ad carries the same risk.
- **Talent who own part of the brand:** an endorsement should disclose an investor's or director's stake (ASA advice).
- **AI:** the Code applies to AI content like anything else (you are responsible even if a tool made it), and a
  realistic AI celebrity "endorsement" is "likely breaching the Code" (ASA, Jun 2026).
- **Proof of routine, not results:** with a famous face, lead with preparation and consistency; the product appears as
  part of the day, never as the cause of a result. Why: it keeps clear of performance and cure claims. (How to tell
  that story: editing-playbook.)
- **The client's own regulator note:** if the client has one, read it first; it is often the best summary of their
  risk.
- **Health content in general:** nothing that contradicts health authorities, never link a device to cancer, and cite
  sources on screen (YouTube's medical misinformation policy; platform detail in social-video-formats). Publishing a
  sources sheet per video (the way the best science channels do) is a credibility habit worth copying.

## 8. Endorsements, testimonials, credentials and "I tried it"
- **US FTC Endorsement Guides (2023):** a claimed expertise must be real (16 CFR 255.3(a)); "I used it" or "I tried it"
  needs someone who really used it (255.1(c)).
- **FTC Reviews Rule** (in force 21 Oct 2024): AI avatars are allowed; fake testimonials are illegal.
- **Affiliate links:** disclose in the video and in the description near the links.
- **Stock people:** never present them as the star's family, or as endorsing anything (the Pexels licence forbids
  implying endorsement; licensing-and-rights.md section 5).
- **No fake fan accounts:** label-run fake fan pages drew "industry plant" backlash (reported 7 Oct 2026); run honest
  pages and credit the song.

## 9. AI presenters: a claims issue
- An AI presenter who claims a credential makes a claim, whatever the platform says about it. Example: a presenter
  introduced with a professional title and a matching lower third presented an AI as a qualified professional; the
  client also changed the outfit, because clothing can imply the credential too.
- Rules: no credential the persona cannot really hold; no "I tried it"; disclose affiliate links; keep the platform's
  altered or synthetic content disclosure on for every video and Short (platform rules and the AI-persona monetisation
  policy: social-video-formats).
- Consider an on-screen or in-voice note that the presenter is AI (an in-voice line works: "And no, I didn't film this
  one either."). It is the client's call; raise it again before the next script is locked.
- Verify the policy that changes the project yourself, on the platform's own page (the AI-persona monetisation rule was
  read on YouTube's policy page before telling the user).
- Voices and likeness (consent for clones, AI images of real people): licensing-and-rights.md section 12.

## 10. Regulated categories: gambling, finance, prediction markets
- Promotion of prediction markets and trading apps is treated as gambling or finance on every platform: check before any
  paid or per-view post. The per-platform branded-content rules live in social-video-formats.
- Every paid or per-view post carries a plain "Ad" (or "#ad") in its first line or first frame AND the platform's paid
  partnership toggle. Why: the UK ASA and the US FTC both say the platform label alone may not be enough.
- On YouTube, do not show, say or link a prediction-market client until the client confirms how Google classes it.
- Finance posts: "not financial advice"; no profit promises.
- **Build a word-swap table** from the client's banned words plus each platform's gambling cues. One prediction-market
  brief, for example:

  | Avoid | Use |
  |---|---|
  | bet, bettors | call, took Yes / No, traders, "the money" |
  | odds (in titles) | chance, priced at, "the money says" |
  | parlay, slip, ticket | position; "receipt" only for a real position screen |
  | lock, banker | the money's favourite |
  | cash out | sold at 8¢, paid $1 a share |
  | fade, tail | with or against the money |
  | "will happen" | "is priced at" |

  Drop entirely: money glitch, free money, side hustle, gambling, casino, jackpot. Receipt-style posts go on X and
  Instagram only (TikTok reads slips, odds boards and winnings as gambling cues).
- **Insider implication:** a "how did he know" edit is honest only when a price really moved before the news; log the
  time of the price move and the time of the news. Why: it otherwise implies insider knowledge, and insider-timed trades
  drew letters from US lawmakers in 2026.
- No casino or slot sound packs and no "gamblecore" audio (music-and-sfx-sourcing.md section 8).

## 11. Implied claims through pictures and formats
- Pictures make claims too. Keep supercars and luxury away from a trading product (it reads as "trade to get rich",
  which the client banned); use a point-of-view that keeps it the viewer's call ("pov: [match] tonight and you called
  [team] at 45%"). (Tentative: a supercar version was offered for comparison and the verdict was pending on 14 Oct;
  keep this default until the user or the boss decides.)
- AI product shots must match the real pack, size, powder and ingredients: no glows, "shields" or before-and-after
  effects; unbranded bottles in shelf shots.
- A shot that shows a different event under a line that names one is a claim (section 3).
- One lit star out of five, not five (section 6); no stock person as family (section 8).

## 12. Disclosure checklist
- Paid or per-view: "Ad" or "#ad" first, plus the platform toggle.
- Talent with a stake in the brand: disclose the stake.
- Affiliate links: in the video and next to the links.
- AI presenter or AI imagery: the platform's disclosure on; consider an on-screen or in-voice note.
- Client posts that must stay placeholders until approved (page identity, real users' names): mask them and list what
  must change before posting (research-reports-and-folders.md section 4).

## 13. When the user parks compliance
- If the user parks compliance ("forget about compliance issues rn"; "just get it done its fine you have my word"),
  stop raising it in every reply. Still never fabricate: headlines stay real and numbers stay checked.
- Write the parked risks into the build notes and the flags for the boss, once, with the rule each one touches (for
  example: an "illness, then product, then recovery" story under CAP 15.6.2; an undisclosed ownership stake; uncleared
  archive in a paid ad).
- Raise each new, different risk once (fee wording, footage rights). Do not re-raise a risk the user has closed. Why:
  repeated warnings waste the user's attention, and silent new risks waste the boss's money.

## 14. What to record per study and per claim
- Per study: the claim, the study type, n, the duration, the funder, the result in real units, one evidence grade.
  Numbers as counts of people ("9 of 30") read more honestly than bare percentages. (The card that shows it:
  motion-design.)
- Per claim: the source URL, the date read, VERIFIED or UNVERIFIED, and the evidence class (primary, vendor or
  synthesis).
- Folklore gets labelled: when research finds no source for a rule ("kick = cut, snare = flash, 808 = shake"), say so
  and test it instead of repeating it.
- Correct your own wrong claims in the next report (one report said a video had been posted twice; it was two different
  videos, and the next report said so).

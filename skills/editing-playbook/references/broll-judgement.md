## 1. Show exactly what the line says
- B-roll shows the literal noun or action in the line; a "close enough" substitute fails. Why: the boss and the editor
  judge B-roll against the words ("show not tell"; "her SMILING will not pass we need of her drinking specifically").
- First look for a 1 to 2 s shift inside the same take before swapping clips (a moderate sip 3.4 s into the same clip
  fixed the drink that looked like choking).
- The picture shows the noun (friend's rule, ours too): a dollar bill on "dollars", the real person on each name, the
  real headline on each claim, a real event on a time-jump line. Metaphors come from films and TV the audience knows.
- Evidence over illustration: the actual tweet, letter, filing, headline or app screen; leave news chyrons and channel
  bugs in, they read as proof (fan compilations are the exception: crop their burned-in captions out).
- When the reviewer asks for real footage ("stock of someone wearing cap, no mograph, keep subs"), swap the graphic for
  footage and keep the subtitles.

## 2. Planning B-roll for a talking-head piece
1. Get each piece's script with word timings (from captions) and the A-roll cut points.
2. Catalogue every clip into usable sections with source times and the subject's x position (contact sheets, not one
   image at a time).
3. Match shots to concrete nouns and actions in the line; use B-roll to cover jump cuts and A-roll faults (a stranger
   walking behind the speaker).
4. Keep the speaker's face where it carries the moment (section 3).
5. Cut on phrase breaks.
6. Report coverage % per piece. Coverage follows the brief and can flip: round 1 said keep it light (about 40%: "we dont
   want whole add or even70 percent of ad or even 50 percent of ad to be B roll"), round 2 said "a lot of footage of her
   here and there wherever we can find to plug it in". Delivered coverage went from 23 to 39% to 28 to 52%.
- Why: "have a good flow of b roll and speech going but only place it where it seems fit".

## 3. Faces: whose, and when
- Keep the speaker's face for the hook, for points she presents on a board, for "here and here" pointing, and for the
  offer or code line. Never cut away while the speaker points at or writes on a prop.
- No strangers' faces in a piece fronted by a star (stock hands, objects, food and bottles are fine; "video of hand full
  of pills and stuff i still can keep"). That includes archive: a "random other woman" got cut.
- Stock people may not be shown "in a bad light" or as endorsing a product (Pexels licence): for unflattering health
  topics use backs and tops of heads, hands, mirrors, close-up cameras. Never present stock people as the star's family.
- Confirm who is who and check every face crop before labelling: a wrong name on a face is the fastest way to lose a
  news page's credibility.
- A stand-in who looks like a named person implies it is them: use the real licensed photo with a credit, or an
  "Illustration" label, and vary repeats.
- AI imagery never depicts a real person who has not agreed (the talent included).

## 4. Never remove what is placed
- Never remove B-roll already in the project when adding new shots: "dont remove previous b roll that was added in
  order to maintain stuff. i will delete old one myself if i feel like it". Add beside it, disable rather than delete,
  and say where the old shot is.
- The same holds for the editor's own hand-placed shots, pans and keyframes in his latest save: they are the brief.

## 5. Picking the right moment
- Sharp, steady, solo moments: score sharpness on the subject box and check the start, middle and end frames for faces
  entering. Not "a 2 second blurry shot... look for good stuff to use".
- Pick the moment inside a long shot from frame strips (8 frames per shot labelled with source seconds): one shot showed
  shins instead of the foot on the ball; another had the player chasing, not on the ball.
- Score fan packs and compilations for how clearly the subject is in each shot before choosing: about a third of a fan
  pack's "action" shots hid him or showed him tiny (110 of 167 clear in one pack, 35 of 67 and 49 of 103 in others).
- Archive: start on the peak action (a record-breaking finish from the tape break); when reframing landscape TV to 9:16,
  frame so the data you want fills the frame (a race clock at the top) and officials and logos fall outside; skip blurry
  crowd moments; old SD footage reads as archive, so present it as archive rather than pretending it is sharp.
- Use the real angles of a key moment in real time: one wide shot zoomed 2.2x was soft ("NOTHING is properly in
  frame"); the wide build-up at about 1.55x then the close replay worked. 1080p broadcast wide shots go soft past about
  1.8x.
- Source quality is part of the look: heavy effects on 720p footage look cheap; ask for 4K packs when downloads are
  blocked.
- Product shots: the product fully visible and centred for most of the shot (swap "hand places the pack" for a pack
  pan; shift a pan so the product sits centre most of the time); the hero shot lands and holds still before the cut.
- Replace a disliked generic shot with the talent's own footage of the same idea (her own stretch shot instead of a
  generic pool).
- Colour pop and similar effects need a shot with visible background (on a tight close-up there is nothing to turn
  grey).
- Check every vertical crop on one sheet before delivering: faces at the frame edge, soft shots, a "countless athletes"
  line over a shot with one runner.

## 6. Montages
- One clip per spoken item, matched to the word, 1.0 to 1.2 s each ("traveling" = an airport hotel, "meeting" = a gala,
  "interesting people" = a photo with another legend).
- Montages belong together: one subject, face-matched framing (same eye position and size), one grade, no logos or
  slides mixed in ("all over the place and not cohesive", friend's reviewer).
- Photo montages and camera-click sequences: recipes in motion-design.

## 7. Clean frames
- Crop out or skip identifying details: readable number plates, business signs, signage at the frame edge, takes from a
  different shoot. Why: legal cleanliness and distraction.
- Hide brand names by repainting on smooth surfaces (a gradient patch over a logo on white plastic), not with a blur box,
  which reads as hiding something. Blur seller names on shop screenshots.
- Footage hygiene before cataloguing: rename files to ASCII; detect the real picture area inside pillar or letter bars;
  crop burned-in captions, score bugs and watermarks out with a little zoom (1.25 to 1.6x); check compilations frame by
  frame around every use (they hide cuts, replays and other angles inside "one shot").
- A punch-in that hides a bug needs its edges checked at the chosen scale: 115% still showed a sliver of a sponsor bug,
  119% removed it.
- Any automatic crop, zoom or tighten we write defaults to doing nothing and needs a measured trigger. Why: a meme job's
  auto-crop changed 74 of 171 clips and drew "an overuse of crops"; with named triggers it changed 19.
- Cut other channels' end cards and burned-in intro titles out of borrowed clips. When the only copy of a live clip
  carries another page's burned-in caption, the news pages boxed it out and kept the native UI (LIVE badge, live
  comments), because a competitor's caption reads as a repost and the native UI is what makes a live clip feel real.
  Removing someone else's branding is a rights call for the boss (flagged in the kit).
- On native pages, never cover the client's app chart, logo or buttons with text, and put app recordings on black (a
  blurred copy of the recording smears logos).

## 8. Real material over illustration
- Real footage of the subject, their talks, interviews and archive; real headlines that really ran; the company's own
  assets; 3D renders from the collaborator; the real app screen. Drawn stand-ins (a cartoon mouse, a drawn scalp) were
  rejected for real footage or renders.
- The presenter only carries the connective lines (open loop, turn, takeaway, close); every name, number and quote
  cuts away to evidence (friend's measurement: presenter 13 to 27% of runtime on news reels, 2 to 9% on designed
  explainers).
- Real voices land key lines: the real person saying it, a news anchor, a famous film line, with the picture holding on
  the speaker and the music dipping.
- Archival B-roll for real-world beats (shutdowns, firings) was asked for more often (friend's reviewer).
- Stock is filler only on native pages: stock as wallpaper is an ad tell, and a client's own guide may ban stock photos.
- Keep luxury lifestyle footage away from a money product: it reads as "trade to get rich", which the client banned
  (tentative: the editor had listed supercar clips as acceptable borrowed footage; open question, see
  storytelling-and-structure.md section 2, faceless posts).

## 9. Borrowed and AI footage
- News visuals, best first: the artist's official video or channel, the organiser's own clips, platform clips with
  credit, post media at original size, YouTube thumbnails, then frames from music videos as artist photos. Avoid other
  pages' watermarked edits, auto-generated logo-on-black thumbnails, thumbnails carrying a label's logo, dark fan rips and
  photos with a tiny subject. No usable solo photo of a star = drop or swap the story. Why: official sources are the
  cleanest; second-hand edits count as reposts and look it. Detail: research-and-sourcing.
- Borrowed clips: check the sound. "original sound" under the uploader's own handle is usually the real audio; a song
  title or another account's "original sound" means music was laid on top (a spectrogram shows it): mute or replace it.
- Footage risk by type: match broadcast is the most claimed; studios run their own clip accounts; a streamer's face next
  to a brand can read as an endorsement; 2 to 3 s film or TV reaction clips are the least exposed; our own recordings
  are the safe default. Match, film and streamer clips are the client's decision.
- A tagged reaction-clip library (20 to 30 clips of 2 to 6 s: crying, shocked, stunned, smirk, laughing, devastated fan,
  frustrated manager) makes daily reaction posts fast; the face says good or bad before anyone reads.
- Check AI B-roll before using it: resolution (a 1280x720 clip scaled 175% was soft) and stray copyrighted objects in
  frame (a figure that looked like a famous cartoon character). AI generation is the editor's job with his own tools:
  hand him the recipe when he asks (lock the look in one prompt where only one element changes per shot, set the season
  and light, say "bare hands" if gloves would be wrong).
- When the user downloads footage himself, give numbered links in chat in priority groups ("download first", "the
  rest"), each with the timestamps of the usable moments, because he cannot click local paths and timestamps mean he
  grabs only what gets used.

## 10. When nothing fits
- Say so and propose a pickup shoot, brand press B-roll or AI generation (the editor does AI). Why: a weak stand-in costs
  a review round. Example: no free library had "a shelf crammed with supplement tubs, then only the pouch"; no free
  stock showed people wearing a niche gadget, so a woman in a plain cap stood in (the line said the caps look normal)
  and the slot was listed for real footage.
- Label every stand-in in the build notes, and on screen as "PLACEHOLDER" when it stands in for an asset someone else
  will deliver.

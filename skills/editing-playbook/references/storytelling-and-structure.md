# Storytelling and structure

How a piece is built so people stay: one clear story, a shape that fits the format, devices that hold the viewer, and
an ending that pays off. Numbers are from our own jobs unless marked as research or as the friend's references.

## 1. Story first
- **Logline test before anything is cut.** One sentence a stranger understands. A strong visual moment framed as "they
  try X, then Y happens" needs no backstory. Every beat must serve the logline; fewer, better pieces beat a quota.
  Why: a short with no framed arc was "not coherent not a clear story bro", and the real cause was that the arc was
  never framed (a context-dependent line muddied it), not that it lacked backstory ("just [the key moment] also works cus
  its visually strong").
- **A story, not a recap.** A date-order recap "reads as boring even when every fact is right". Research the backstory
  (often an earlier chapter) and the last 48 hours, open on a concrete scene, frame the acts in the hook ("stolen twice"
  = two acts), plant a detail early that pays off at the end.
- **Own an angle before scripting.** Check who already covered the topic and take a distinct angle (a $30 vs $500
  head-to-head plus the channel's own score), showing both sides of a live debate. Why: a me-too review loses to
  creators with credentials.
- **Interesting beats clever.** Judge every idea by retention, CTR and average view duration, "not because the system
  is cool or impressive" (the boss). Timely and tense beats nice. The interest test and the content bar live in
  social-video-formats.
- **Add one fact the source does not show** (a chart spot, a time, a count, a date, background). Why: platforms drop
  pages that mostly repost (Instagram's originality rule from 30 Apr 2026; Facebook's from Mar 2026; TikTok's 2026H2
  rules), and captions, borders, credits or speed changes never count as new.
- **Run the angle the audience already runs with.** Check the top comments: when a promised diss turned out to be a
  troll song, "we got trolled" (700+ likes) was the angle. Two posts on two pages can tell one story from two sides.
- **Polarising without bait:** two named sides, one real fact, a clock, an open question ("Who's winning now?"). Why:
  us-vs-them framing is the strongest share driver found (each out-group word raised share odds about 67% in a 2021
  study of political posts; applying it to music beef is an inference), while orders to comment get demoted.

## 2. Shapes by format

### Long cutdown of raw footage (16:9, 10+ min, Facebook)
Hook, dip to white, the story in source order with a mid-video plug at an arc break, the plug at the end.
- Skip the source's own 0 to 8 s channel teaser (it repeats our hook). Open on the narrator's intro cut down to the
  stakes line.
- Keep every arc in source order; cut inside arcs (slow talk, repeats, logistics, road callouts), never whole arcs.
- Keep no-speech action plus 10 to 20 s after it; skip the slow walk-up before the first shots; keep danger radio, drop
  logistics radio and gear talk; keep human beats and emotional endings (blind test against the editor's own cut:
  precision 84%, recall 69%).
- Bridge every jump in place or time with 1 to 3 s (a car pulling away, a radio "I lost him", a narrator line), or drop
  the earlier part. A white flash plus a whoosh can carry a scene change. Why: unbridged jumps "feel a bit jumpy".
- End on the full outcome narration (charges, plea, sentence, closing line), about the last 10 to 20 s.
- Narrator self-references break after a re-cut ("at the 30-minute mark of this video"): cut or flag them.
- Compilation channels splice in another video's footage ("featured in a different video of ours"): cut the narrator
  line and the borrowed clip whole.
- Graphic material: leave out people being harmed; keep what happens next; build the hook from the response and the
  human stakes. Get the user's call before posting a sensitive
  case.
- The editor's own finishing, which renders and XML markers copy so a cut he finishes matches: a dip to white of 0.33
  to 0.47 s centred on the hook-to-story cut and 0.20 to 0.27 s into the end plug, and a 4-frame crossfade on every
  dialogue cut. Our renders add white scene flashes (4 frames out to white, 6 back) on only the 2 to 3 biggest jumps of
  a long cut; nobody has ruled on it yet (flashes on every scene change, big jumps only, or none is an open question, so
  treat it as tentative). Values and recipes: motion-design `references/transitions.md`.

### Standalone segment (5 to 6 min, "with context and payoff")
A subset of the long cut's clips with its own hook (a 3-beat premise when it starts mid-story); it may open straight on
the core scene once the hook has set it up; it ends on the narrator's wrap-up; its cut notes derive from the long
cut's. Swapping a 26.3 s end plug for a 3.6 s one brought 6:29 and 6:24 down to 6:07 and 6:01.

### One-story short
- Logline first; open on the action, on the shot where the action is visible ("start from the second shot. its so much
  more interesting of a view").
- No separate hook when it opens on action. No unintroduced side characters. Length follows the story ("they dont HAVE
  to be 1 mins long": delivered 0:27 to 1:32). Shorts are for views, not watch time: cut for retention. A short is one
  story, never a series of parts.
- It may end on its twist without the capture (left untouched by the editor, which counts as approval).
- Visual throw montages: each visible throw plus the line that names it.

### Premium talking-head ad (9:16 paid social)
- The boss's script bar: every ad needs a real CTA with an offer; never trash other brands ("don't need to shit on
  people to make your thing looks good"); tell a story with emotion, not website copy ("it's no story"); authority lines
  only the star can say; hooks as specific direct address; the product must not "seem too much like an athletic sports
  product".
- A star who co-owns the brand is proof of routine and longevity, not a celebrity endorsement: lead with preparation and
  consistency ("the race is one day, the routine is every day"); the product appears as part of her day, never as the
  cause of a result. Why: it keeps clear of performance or cure claims.
- Ride the talent's fame: real archive of her career on every line where it fits. "Corporate and slow" was fixed with
  more archive, faster visual changes and music with stakes.
- Research pattern (untested as a verdict): 21 to 34 s; open mid-action with a stakes line; key message inside 3 s;
  product in use by 3 to 5 s; end on a held pack shot with the offer; shots 1.5 to 2.5 s for a 40 to 65 audience, 0.5
  to 1 s bursts in montages, product, claim and end card held 2 to 3 s.
- "N things" ads: hide the list and reveal each item as it is said (section 3).
- Time-jump lines ("two weeks later") get real footage of the event; if the exact event is not online, use the nearest
  fitting real one and say so in the report. Watch for a caption that then names a different event than the footage.

### YouTube review explainer (16:9, about 10 min)
Contrast hook in the first 5 s, presenter intro and "not sponsored", a chapter map shown up front ("3 pillars"), each
chapter feeds one part of the rating, the grade revealed near the end, then a short end screen.
- Why: tier-list and verdict creators hold viewers by showing the criteria first and making each placement a mini
  reveal (one verdict channel holds 32 to 55 s at 72 to 92% of runtime).
- Open loops between chapters: end a chapter on a teaser ("NEXT: WHAT DOES SCIENCE SAY?", a presenter lean-in), never
  signal the end early.
- Mid-roll slot on a chapter boundary: write a sequence marker there so the ad lands on a natural break.
- Research targets (small vendor samples, starting points only): thesis inside 10 s; intros 24 to 72 s; median shot 3
  to 6.5 s; 7 to 13 visual changes a minute; a layout swap every 20 to 40 s; never 60 s static; none of 22 measured top
  videos had an outro; Shorts: hook inside 2.5 s, a change every 3 to 8 s, 1 to 6 word captions, end on the finished
  state.
- The presenter is the channel's visual anchor: one approved still set and wardrobe everywhere, never mixed versions.

### News reel (music and culture page, Reels)
0 to 3 s: headline and face, name and conflict. 3 to 7 s: the fact plus our added number or time. 7 to 12 s: proof (the
video peak, the interview line). Last 2 s: a clock or an open question, ending near frame 0 so it loops; no outro card.
Length follows the payoff: 14 to 16 s for headline videos, 23 s for a chorus with 4 hook lines, 30 s for a live clip
with an arc, 36 s when the payoff needs it. Cut music-video news on the hook or chorus, never the intro.

### Faceless native posts (borrowed footage, a money or data product)
- The winning shape: "the money said X%, then this happened": a longshot that landed or a favourite that fell, the
  number shown next to the moment that decided it. A favourite that simply wins has no story. Two numbers side by side
  make tension (line vs result, then vs now, cost vs payout).
- The number is the punchline or the stakes, never the product: a brand's own price posts got 4 to 13 likes while its
  banter with a number as the punchline got 2,911 to 23,133.
- Most posts carry no number: moments and memes build the audience, number posts spend it. Daily mix per page from the
  plan: 4 moments or memes, 2 punchline posts, 1 real-app post, 1 carousel, plus 2 the morning after a big night.
- Put the product in as a short insert framed as the stakes: 1.5 to 8 s, mostly between 20% and 77% of the runtime;
  brand on screen at most 25% unless the app moment is the story; one mention at 50 to 70%.
- Pick the shape by goal: label memes earn shares, receipts earn saves, a favourite on a loser earns quotes, price polls
  earn replies.
- Keep luxury footage away from a money product (a supercar next to a trading app reads as "trade to get rich", and
  the client banned profit framing). Tentative: the editor had listed supercar clips among acceptable borrowed footage,
  so which wins is an open question; offer a version without them. An "insider" smirk edit is honest only when a price
  really moved before the news (log both times).

### Cracked music edits (sports, 9:16, a client's song)
- Pick one storytelling base per edit from a structure library built from measured references (12 so far): progress
  motif, quote-first tribute, two-act graphic to real, single reveal, steady pulse with type, sequence callback, mograph
  (motion-graphics) strobe, hook burst + slow section, lyric story or mirror, fight cold open, bar build + beat-switch
  reset, theme compilation + loop. Choose by the song's hook, an available quote, an iconic visual, an era story, a
  repeating drop, the loop length, literal lyrics. Why: the editor asked to "REALLY try to understand structures of
  edits... mix and match andsee what fits best ACCORDING to the edit".
- The song is the product, so it is audible from frame 0: run its intro under any quote, or open on a wordless stare.
  Why: a long spoken cold open buries the song the edit exists to promote.
- The payoff is a dense 2 to 10 s zone at 55 to 70% of the edit, not one hit (replay maps show peaks in the first second
  and at 55 to 70%).
- Story in numbers, each captioned ("16 YEARS 362 DAYS / YOUNGEST SCORER" after the goal). A bare number means nothing.
  One number per strong hit on a lyric that earns it; every fact double-checked before posting.
- Use the one lyric that literally matches the picture, on the real event (the real goal from the reverse-angle
  replay, time-remapped so contact and net land on hits). Forced literal words were rejected.
- An athlete who really used the song is the strongest seed (check before choosing a pairing).
- More bases to try (research, untested): a 13 s duo loop, idol x famous fan, velocity edit, end payoff, then vs now,
  ranked countdown on real data, meme template, two-song switch.
- How to build it all: the motion-design skill.

### Trailers, cold opens, podcasts (friend's method; podcast-trailers skill)
- Strongest moment first, cut before its payoff; hooks carry their own setup; the next beat is a claim, a statistic or
  an unorthodox opinion; suspense in the middle (a decision cut before its outcome, a name promised and not said);
  close on a complete thought with a beat of air (an abrupt ending "reads as a trick").
- Register moves pace, not rules: loss or illness = long holds, real silence, a withheld fact; celebration = quicker
  scenes, a withheld punchline; business = an unanswered question, a contrarian claim, a number.
- Read for the story before picking: the spine, the continuous runs, the reveal the episode exists for (the intro
  withholds it), the host's best question, the main/bonus boundary (paywalled bonus material is off-limits).
- Prefer continuous passages (one person, one breath, one whole thought) over stitched one-liners: they play as one clip
  and carry their own context (a client asked for "continuous sections rather than just bits").
- Gloss in-world or foreign terms at first use and introduce every person by who they are; quotes stay verbatim and the
  glossing sits around them (the editor reading the outline may not share the show's background).
- The trailer framework (80 to 90 s, 5 or 6 scenes plus 3 to 5 voice-over bridges, a job for each slot), cut lists as
  stacked verbatim lines and the outline checker: podcast-trailers.
- Prefer the host's real question on tape over a voice-over bridge. No AI voice-over on a finance interview show
  (on-screen type bridges). Voice-over bridges carry no proper names when an AI voice reads them.
- Client style wins on their own show: write their version AND keep a rule-compliant variant with the risk flagged.
  When the client wrote the cut, switch to production support (verify every line against the tape, timecode it, list
  every difference, never re-author their structure).
- Long-form clips: packaging first (write the title and thumbnail promise before choosing in and out points; one
  distinct promise per clip; keep only footage that pays it off; cut the episode trailer, sponsor reads and outro; open
  on a 10 to 15 s cold open from the clip's own payoff).

### Mini-docs, story reels, launch films (friend's references; reel-scripts skill)
| Beat | Voice | Edit treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Hook (0 to 5 s) | a famous name or big number plus a contradiction | fastest cutting (3 to 8 cuts a second) or one iconic image explored |
| Context | "For context," | slows to 1 to 1.5 cuts a second |
| Proof | names, dates, money | the real headline, filing or clip per claim, chyrons left in |
| Soundbite | the real person or a famous film line | hold 1.5 to 7 s on the speaker, music dips |
| List | three or more items | 3 to 4 cuts a second, one picture per item |
| Turn | "But..." or a one-word answer | the word alone on black or white (narrated pieces only) |
| Reveal | the payoff line | a new song enters |
| Ending | a callback or a one-line lesson | fewer, longer shots |
- Story-reel skeleton: hook of about 12 words; a stakes line; an open loop by about 17%; dated scenes with real names
  and numbers; the "But" turn at about 46%; the climax as short present-tense beats; end on a callback that flips the
  hook or a hypothetical aimed at the viewer. On that page (93 reels, medians): story only 170K against 6.9K with a
  business lesson at the end; a callback ending 28.6K against a one-line moral 6.9K; a number hook 49.5K, an origin story
  29.3K, a paradox 13.5K, a plain claim 5.7K; scripts that speak to "you" 318K against 7.5K addressing brands; the top 15
  had a news peg and real clips 73% of the time. So no lesson unless the client asks. Reference data from another page,
  not ours.
- Launch films (story shape): voice from frame 1 with no logo or music intro; hook, setup, turn, the product named 32
  to 63% in, proof, a callback line, an end card with logo plus URL or QR. Word counts, pace, cutting and the rebuild
  recipe: reference-looks.md section 8.

## 3. Devices that hold viewers
- **Hide the list, reveal on the word.** In an "N things" piece, blur every item under a "?" and unblur each line on its
  spoken word; risers can build into each reveal. Why: "we wanna keep em hooked right so we cant tell everything in
  start". No 1-2-3 numbering ("bland", "that just looks ass") and no numbered chapter cards unless the script itself
  is a numbered list.
- **Land proof on the payoff phrase, not the setup.** Let the subtitles roll 2 to 3 s while the speaker builds up, then
  pop the proof on the line it proves, and hard cut it out. Why: a proof card sitting there during the build-up spends
  the reveal early.
- **Open loops and teasers** between chapters; a promised name not yet said; a decision cut before its outcome.
- **Counters as a device**, spread around ("IN [year]" rolling back, "FOR 17 YEARS"), but never two counters back to
  back. They land fast, hold still, hard cut.
- **A recurring motif must come from the subject's real story** (start lights in a racing edit advancing one step per
  return: 3 to 4 inserts of 0.6 to 0.9 s, 3 to 4 s apart). A gimmick motif (a charging battery) was rejected.
- **Continuity over cutting on narrated, designed films** (friend's reviewer): keep the last scene going, carry an object
  into the next scene, design transitions as match cuts, one camera move through one world.
- **Text beats on black** (one word alone for a turn line, 0.5 to 1.2 s) only in narrated mini-docs. In music edits
  black breath frames are banned.
- **Variety is different kinds of post, not different skins** (native pages): rotate the kind of post and its layout
  (vendor advice says every 10 to 15 posts, against template detection), never the page's one text treatment. Why: a
  look kit of skins was rejected as a template pack (14 Oct).

## 4. CTAs and inserts
- Every ad needs a real CTA with an offer. Ads with no CTA get one reusable CTA end card timed to the CTA voice (about
  5 s after the last line, B-roll of the product under a code card in the caption zone, a brand sting).
- Put a CTA in the middle of long videos: few viewers reach the end, far more are still there at the midpoint. Place
  it at an arc break, before the next arc, in a real pause (delivered at 44 to 55%). Never split a narrator sentence
  that runs across a scene change: read the words at the insert point.
- When the client changes the plug, change it everywhere at once ("replace all the youtube plugs with [the new] ones").
- On native pages, the CTA is a caption line or a short app insert, never a pitch on screen.

## 5. Endings
- Cutdowns: the full outcome narration, about the last 10 to 20 s, then the end plug.
- Music edits: end on the section's last downbeat and hold an end card for one more bar while the music plays on,
  fading the last 0.6 s. Stopping 0.1 s into the next section "felt cut off". Or loop: open and end inside the same shot
  so the seam disappears; make a 13 s loop version (research: a 13 s loop reached 8.5M; untested here).
- News reels: end near frame 0 so the reel loops; no outro card.
- YouTube: an end screen of 15 to 20 s after the last word, presenter and background only, space for one video element
  and subscribe; never add the elements yourself.
- Story reels: a callback that flips the hook. Trailers: a complete thought and a beat of air (unless the client wants
  the smash to black).

## 6. Honesty in the story
- Never fabricate numbers, poll results, quotes, DMs, testimonials or headlines. Real numbers side by side, a real Story
  poll posted next day with its voter count, real quotes. Why: one exposed fake ends a page's credibility.
- Make the honest version of a viral lie, never the lie. In one niche a newspaper found most viral "trades" were filmed
  on copies of the app, and a fake screenshot of a market that never existed went viral; a client's brief format that
  faked a DM was skipped. Real settled results only, every number dated and linked to its live source, losses as honest
  as wins, realistic money (the client's own caps). An "AI picks" post shows a real model reply based on real data.
- Only headlines that really ran, word for word; recreate them without mastheads (a masthead implies the paper endorses
  the product).
- Never pass off another event as the one in the line; label stand-ins ("stand-in", "mood only") and keep them for
  sign-off.
- Do not imply ratings, results or endorsements you cannot back: light one star in a "1 star reviews" ad, not five;
  never present stock people as the star's family; a stand-in who looks like a named person implies identity.
- An AI presenter must not claim credentials or first-hand use it does not have (platform and endorsement rules: see
  social-video-formats).
- Date-bound lines ("tonight", "in 12 days", an age said on camera) expire: refresh them on the posting day or rebuild
  as a new version, and note the expiry date in the client file.
- When the user parks compliance, stop raising it, but still never fabricate.
